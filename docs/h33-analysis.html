These are counts and set relations, not DTI scores. The byte-level audit indicates H33 is a sparse sample of the mapped-fault halo with the closest distance shells removed. The artifact itself does not establish that those dots correspond to undiscovered faults or geothermal vents.

A plausible metric explanation, not a causal finding

The local evaluator implements a pooled DTI whose true-positive credit saturates per truth cell, while prediction mass and false-positive penalties depend on emitted dots. Consequently, spreading fixed prediction mass across distinct truth neighborhoods can outperform crowding many dots into the same neighborhoods. Removing dots very close to known traces can also reduce wasted prediction mass when the hidden positives do not follow those traces exactly.

That algebra makes sparse coverage a plausible explanation for why the archived H33 pattern might score well. It does not identify why the organizer returned the user-reported 0.2778: the hidden labels, a controlled organizer ablation, and the corresponding submission receipt are unavailable. Other explanations—including label construction, mask rules, and the distribution of hidden traces— remain possible. The physical interpretation is especially limited: the target is fault detection, not a verified map of vents, permeability, fluid temperature, or commercial geothermal resources.

The evaluator implementation is local and version-pinned; for source/manual review see the organizer reference repository and the competition problem page. Availability of the public reference repository does not prove that the historical H33 value came from a particular hidden-label mechanism.
